Think about who a set of brand guidelines was actually written for. A designer at a printer. A new hire. An external agency about to touch the logo. A human, in other words, who already understands design and just needs to know your particular rules.

That reader does most of the work themselves. They know what a logo is for. They understand spacing, hierarchy, when a rule is a rule and when it's a steer. The guidelines only have to fill in the specifics: use this blue, not that one, leave this much space, don't stretch it.

Now a machine is reading the same document. And the machine is the opposite kind of reader.

## It knows the specifics and none of the sense

A human reader brings the understanding and looks up the specifics. The machine arrives with the specifics within reach and none of the understanding behind them.

It can absorb your hex codes instantly. What it can't do is supply the judgement your human readers brought for free. It doesn't know why the logo needs clear space, so it can't tell when crowding it is a mistake. It doesn't know what your brand is trying to feel like, so it treats every rule as equally rigid. It can't tell the difference between a rule that defines you and a convention you'd drop in a heartbeat for a good idea.

Guidelines never had to say any of that, because the reader already knew it. The document was designed around an assumption that no longer holds: that whoever's reading brings the design sense and just needs the house rules.

## What the new reader actually needs

Hand that document to a machine and you get exactly what it was built to produce. Correct specifics, no judgement. It'll match the blue and miss the point.

What the machine needs is the part your guidelines left out because they didn't have to include it. The reasoning under the rules. What the brand is and isn't. Why the choices were made, not just what they were. The thinking a human reader supplied from their own head, written down for a reader that has no such head to draw on.

This isn't a knock on guidelines. They did their job well for the reader they had. But the reader changed, and a document written for someone who already understands design can't instruct something that doesn't.

If a machine is going to act on your brand, it needs a brief written for a machine. Not the logo rules. The understanding the logo rules always took for granted.
