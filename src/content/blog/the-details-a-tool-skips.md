Put a decent piece of work in front of a good creative director and watch where their eye goes. Not to the big idea. They clocked that in the first second. It goes to the small stuff.

The line height that's a touch too tight. The headline that's title-capped on one slide and sentence-capped on the next. The subhead that ends in a full stop when nothing else does. The padding that's six pixels off and makes the whole thing feel slightly unresolved without anyone being able to say why.

None of these sink a piece on their own. Together, they're the difference between work that's fine and work that's finished. And they're exactly what a tool, left to itself, walks straight past.

## Why the small things matter more than they sound

A client rarely points at the line height. They say it looks a bit cheap, or it doesn't feel quite right, and they can't tell you why. The why is almost always an accumulation of small inconsistencies, each one forgivable, adding up to a piece that reads as not-quite-cared-about.

That's the thing about craft at this level. It's invisible when it's right and only felt when it's wrong. Nobody compliments consistent capitalisation. Everybody registers the one headline that breaks it, even if only as a vague unease. The details are doing quiet work whether you attend to them or not.

A fast engine producing volume is a machine for generating these small drifts. Every output is internally plausible and the set doesn't quite line up. One caption ends in a full stop, the next doesn't. One layout breathes, the next is cramped. No single piece is wrong. The body of work is inconsistent, and inconsistency is what reads as amateur.

## The eye that catches them

This is unglamorous work and it's a huge part of why good work looks good. Someone has to care whether the titles end in full stops. Someone has to notice the line height shifted. Someone has to hold the whole body of output to one standard so it hangs together.

A tool won't do it unprompted, because it has no standard of its own to hold the work against. It'll happily produce a hundred nearly-consistent things and call it a day. Catching the drift takes an eye that knows what right looks like and minds when it slips, across everything, not one piece at a time.

That eye used to be a nice-to-have on top of the real work. Now the engine does the production and the eye is the real work. The ideas are getting easier to make. The care that makes them land is as rare as it ever was.

Big ideas get the credit. The details are what people actually feel.
